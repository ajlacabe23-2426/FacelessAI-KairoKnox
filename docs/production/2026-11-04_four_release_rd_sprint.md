## Core Episode 013 — The AI Shopping Trap
**State:** existing approved idea; script below is a revised editorial candidate, *not a replacement* for the prior cut before asset review.
**Length:** ~50–60s. **Audience:** consumers, creators, marketers. **Claim:** AI can narrow choices but does not remove the need to define your priorities.
**Opening-cover options:** "Two Watches. One AI Recommendation." / "AI Picked the Cheapest. Should You?" / "The Product AI Almost Chose for Me."
**First two seconds:** a $29.99 and $359+ watch float beside Kairo. Caption: "SAME QUESTION. DIFFERENT ANSWERS." Prices are illustrative comparisons from prior story; NOT verified live listings.

**Proposed Fraser narration (original):**
"I asked an AI to find me a hiking watch. It put a $29.99 option beside one costing more than $359. Big price difference. But here's what the recommendation didn't settle: Which one actually fits *my* trip? If I need reliable GPS, battery life, and a warranty, the cheapest headline price tells me almost nothing. NielsenIQ says more than half of surveyed U.S. shoppers recently used AI to help them shop. But a separate Gartner survey found far less comfort with letting AI make the purchase. That's the missing step. AI can rank options; it cannot decide what matters to you unless you tell it. Before you buy, check the criteria, source, and trade-offs. So who's responsible for the final decision—you, or your algorithm?"

**Beat/shot map:** 0–3 split-watch hook; 3–12 Kairo search hologram; 12–24 GPS/battery/warranty comparison (do not invent product specifications); 24–36 on-screen NielsenIQ and Gartner attribution (do not equate unlike samples); 36–50 three-check overlay "Criteria · Evidence · Trade-offs"; 50–60 final discussion question.

**Save value:** small 3-question pre-purchase checklist. **Discussion:** "Would you let an AI pay on your behalf, or only compare?"

## Core Episode 014 — THE VISITORS WHO WEREN'T HUMAN (APPROVED; SUPERSEDES 'THE EMPLOYEE YOU NEVER HIRED')
**State:** Approved investigative story, factual source checked November 4, 2026; voice/visual production in progress. **Format:** 9:16 original video, target 75–85 seconds, narrator Fraser.
**Thesis:** Websites built for humans are increasingly encountering agentic browser sessions. Their analytics must separate recorded events from autonomous task completion.
**Source:** Microsoft Clarity research published 2026-10-29, https://clarity.microsoft.com/blog/agentic-analytics-what-agents-do-on-websites/ ; Microsoft follow-up 2026-11-04 https://clarity.microsoft.com/blog/clarity-connects-agents-llms-people/ . Studied **more than 5.2 million** agentic-classified sessions across **230,511** projects from Aug 13–Sep 9, 2026. Agentic sessions CAN INCLUDE human handoffs. Recorded form/checkout/bookings are independent non-mutually-exclusive **events, not proof AI finished transactions**. No claims of malicious bots or autonomous purchases.

**Final narration draft, Fraser:**
"At midnight, every storefront is empty. Yet the visitor counter keeps climbing. Who's browsing? Not always humans. Microsoft Clarity studied over five point two million website sessions involving AI agents, across more than two hundred thirty thousand projects. Some recorded searches, forms, appointment bookings, and checkout events. But there's an important limit: a session involving an agent can also include a human taking over. A checkout event isn't proof that a machine independently completed a purchase. Still, the web is meeting a different kind of visitor: software navigating toward a goal on somebody else's behalf. Imagine a website built to persuade a person. What changes when its next visitor is an agent? And how will a business know whether it served a customer, a helpful assistant, or a mistake? When the visitor isn't always human, the internet needs a new way to understand who's at the door. If AI explores the web for you, what will you stop seeing?"

## Micro Learning 007 — 200 vs 401 vs 403: Read an API Response
**State:** new educational demo; use original local simulation, not a real third-party endpoint.
**Length:** ~50–60s. **Audience:** beginner coders, future SOC/support analysts.
**Promise:** a viewer can distinguish a successful request, missing login and insufficient permission.
**Cover options:** "403 Doesn't Mean Broken." / "One API. Three Responses." / "Login Isn't Permission."

**Proposed Fraser narration (original):**
"Your app makes a request to an API. The server sends back a number. Watch what happens. First, our *simulated* app requests a public lesson: 200 OK. The request worked. Next, we try to open a private lesson without signing in: 401. We need valid authentication. Now we sign in as a viewer—but try an admin-only action. 403 Forbidden. We have an identity; we just don't have permission. That's authentication versus authorization in seconds. Two questions whenever a request fails: Did the server recognize who I am? And is that identity allowed to do this? This is a safe demo, not a live server. Save these three codes: 200, 401, 403. Which one have you seen in the wild?"

**Scene map:** 0–5 code 403 hook; 5–17 button 1 public lesson -> 200; 17–29 button 2 private as guest -> 401; 29–42 button 3 admin as viewer -> 403; 42–53 simple authentication/authorization contrast; 53–60 recap and question.
**Demonstration:** `demos/api-status-lab.html`, self-contained clickable HTML with no network, no credentials, no security bypass. Screen-capture on 9:16 phone safe area. **Source:** MDN HTTP status references. **Save value:** 200=worked / 401=not authenticated / 403=not permitted.
